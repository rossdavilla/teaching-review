### PSY157 · Personal Relationships — 5

**Contextual predictors of state loneliness: The role of social company, activity, location, and duration of contextual exposure**  
*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* · Busch et al. · [10.1037/pspp0000619](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000619)  
→ **L7 Friendship**

Preregistered analysis of 22,031 Day Reconstruction Method episodes from 2,508 Germans aged 17–95, drawn from the German Socio-Economic Panel across four waves. Multilevel logistic models showed being alone consistently raised the odds of feeling lonely, and people were lonelier at home than at work or elsewhere. Watching television raised it; self-care and media use lowered it. Duration mattered too: prolonged care work increased loneliness, while prolonged social contact decreased it.

**Why it matters:** Your slides frame loneliness dispositionally (“often have average # of friends”). This makes it a context-driven state with teachable triggers — and **lonelier at home than at work** is a genuinely counterintuitive opener.

**Which Way Does Cupid’s Arrow Go? Distinguishing Happiness and Meaningfulness Within Romantic Relationships**  
*Journal of Social and Personal Relationships* · Thiruchselvam et al. · [10.1177/02654075261477595](https://doi.org/10.1177/02654075261477595)  
→ **L6 Interdependence**

Two online studies separating relationship happiness from relationship meaningfulness. In Study 1 (n = 426), greater sexual desire and satisfaction and fewer sacrifices and less stress predicted happiness but were unrelated to meaningfulness; greater negative self-disclosure to a partner predicted meaningfulness but not happiness. Study 2 (n = 711) replicated this and found that people who prefer a meaningful relationship over a happy one report higher satisfaction, optimism, closeness and forgiveness, and less stress.

**Why it matters:** Your Social Exchange slides treat satisfaction as one quantity (Outcomes vs. CL). This splits the outcome in two and shows the costs that **reduce happiness can build meaning** — a direct complication of the rewards/costs ledger.

**Is Authenticity Perceptible? Accuracy in Judgments of State Authenticity From Expressive Behavior**  
*Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin* · Castro et al. · [10.1177/01461672261481957](https://doi.org/10.1177/01461672261481957)  
→ **L4 Social Cognition**

Five studies testing whether people can actually detect when someone else feels authentic. In acquainted dyads, romantic partners read each other accurately after making sacrifices (Study 1) and after discussing personal distress and desired change (Study 2), but failed when discussing relationship conflict (Study 3) — the authors attribute this to the high interpersonal stakes. In Studies 4A and 4B, outside observers watching thin-slice videos of those same conflict conversations did detect the differences the partners had missed.

**Why it matters:** A clean boundary condition for your “Accuracy of Partner Perceptions” slides, and the thin-slice result links back to the **0.1-second first impression** material earlier in the same lecture.

**Extending Truth-Default Theory to Misinformation: Connecting Misinformation Scholarship and Deception Research**  
*Perspectives on Psychological Science* · Moore & Hancock · [10.1177/17456916261475589](https://doi.org/10.1177/17456916261475589)  
→ **L10 Stresses · L11 Persuasion** · also PSY170

Connects truth-default theory, developed in deception research, to the misinformation literature. TDT predicts findings misinformation scholars found surprising — notably that relatively few people are exposed to or share misinformation online. It also cuts the other way: TDT's claim that people are reflexively truth-biased is challenged by misinformation results. The authors argue deception-detection paradigms clarify how detection studies should be designed (true–false base rates, prompted versus unprompted judgments) and how to improve detection without eroding trust in credible news.

**Why it matters:** You already teach truth bias and the finance-lie study where 0% detected deception. This **names the theory** organizing those findings and bridges to material students care about.

**Self-Esteem as a Shared Resource Beyond Romantic Relationships: An Extension Into Friendships and Parental Relationships**  
*Journal of Social and Personal Relationships* · Hoplock et al. · [10.1177/02654075261498961](https://doi.org/10.1177/02654075261498961)  
→ **L7 Friendship · L4**

Introductory psychology students at a Canadian university each recruited a significant other — a romantic partner, a close friend, or a primary caregiver — giving 308 dyads who completed self-esteem and relationship-quality measures. Actor–Partner Interdependence analyses replicated Robinson and Cameron's 2012 romantic finding and extended it: the higher the two members' combined self-esteem, the higher the quality of the relationship at the dyad level. Self-esteem appears to work as a shared resource across all three relationship types, not just romantic ones.

**Why it matters:** Your partner-feedback material (self-verification vs. self-enhancement) is romantic-only. This **licenses extending it to friendship**, where Lecture 7 already lives.

### PSY170 · Industrial/Organizational Psychology — 5

**A meta-analytic examination of the criterion-related validity and cognitive loading associated with traditional and new situational judgment test paradigms**  
*Journal of Applied Psychology* · Lievens et al. · [10.1037/apl0001420](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0001420)  
→ **L2 Selection**

Meta-analysis of 457 independent samples from 302 studies, total N = 244,257. Situational judgment tests predicted not only job performance but also academic performance, organizational citizenship behavior and counterproductive work behavior, and validity was higher in real applicant settings than in research settings. Newer formats held up: multimedia SJTs matched text-based validity at lower cognitive load, multiple-choice did not outperform written constructed responses, and webcam-recorded responses carried the lowest cognitive load of all. The authors discuss cost-effectiveness against other selection methods.

**Why it matters:** Your selection lecture covers biodata, work samples, references, background checks, cybervetting and interviews — **but not SJTs**, now among the best-evidenced tools available. Your closing slide asks how interviews can be improved; this is the missing third answer, with the largest N in the batch.

**Industrial-organizational science that matters: Social impact and policy implications in the Journal of Applied Psychology**  
*Journal of Applied Psychology* · Casper et al. · [10.1037/apl0001425](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0001425)  
→ **L1 Intro · L13 Future**

BERTopic modeling of 5,865 empirical articles published in the Journal of Applied Psychology from its 1917 founding through June 2024, yielding 99 topics across 25 higher-order themes. Five themes bear directly on social impact: workplace stress and well-being; diversity, equity and inclusion; organizational justice and legal issues; employment transitions across the lifespan; and social justice and labor opinions. Trends from 1980 to 2024 show health and well-being research growing, DEI scholarship holding steady, justice and legal topics rising then falling, and labor-related research disappearing altogether.

**Why it matters:** A far better “History of I/O” slide than Yerkes and WW1 testing alone — a data-driven century of the field’s own priorities. The **vanishing of labor research** also speaks to your Lecture 5 claim about pensions and union power.

**Daily job crafting with generative artificial intelligence: Implications for work meaningfulness and engagement**  
*Journal of Vocational Behavior* · Güven et al. · [10.1016/j.jvb.2026.104297](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvb.2026.104297)  
→ **L13 Future · L4 Motivation**

Ten-day daily diary study of 149 UK full-time employees who used generative AI at work daily or more (mean age 36.7, 63.8% male), building on self-determination theory and the job crafting literature. Four crafting dimensions — augmenting instrumental resources, augmenting social resources, augmenting challenging demands, and automating hindering demands — were linked to daily engagement through need satisfaction and then work meaningfulness. The competence pathway drew the most consistent support and relatedness the least. In open-ended responses, employees described automating drudgery as a deliberate reallocation of attention toward work they valued rather than simply doing less.

**Why it matters:** Your AI lecture rests on E0/E1/E2 exposure and EPOCH, both conceptual. This is **empirical evidence about what employees actually do with AI**, and it maps onto the Job Characteristics Model you already teach.

**Are the basic psychological needs of self-determination theory necessary for employee well-being?**  
*Journal of Vocational Behavior* · Ding et al. · [10.1016/j.jvb.2026.104308](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvb.2026.104308)  
→ **L4 Motivation**

Tests a cornerstone claim that self-determination theory has asserted for three decades but never properly examined: that autonomy, competence and relatedness are *necessary* for well-being, not merely correlated with it. The authors argue regression-based methods cannot answer this because they express an average-effect logic, and instead apply Necessary Condition Analysis. Across three studies all three needs were confirmed as necessary for evaluative well-being (job satisfaction and work meaningfulness), affective well-being (positive affect) and conative well-being (work engagement), with some variation in how strongly. Power and status were not necessary.

**Why it matters:** This **strengthens** your SDT slide rather than undermining it, and gives a sharp methods aside: “necessary” and “correlated on average” are different claims needing different statistics.

**A meta-analysis of the dynamism of emergent states and team processes**  
*Journal of Applied Psychology* · Loignon et al. · [10.1037/apl0001407](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0001407)  
→ **L8 Groups**

Cross-lagged meta-analytic structural equation models synthesizing over 3,000 correlations from longitudinal studies, representing more than 11,000 teams. Emergent states and processes showed inertia, but unevenly: conflict was the stickiest construct and affect the least. Team affect and cognition tended to coevolve with team processes, while motivation and conflict were consistently predicted by earlier processes. Team performance formed feedback loops, predicting later states and processes which in turn predicted later performance. The interval between observations moderated all of this.

**Why it matters:** Your teamwork material is static — problems and fixes. **“Conflict is the stickiest thing in a team”** is memorable and actionable next to your social-loafing remedies.

### PSY175 · Human Sexuality — 6

**Sex Education in the Age of Artificial Intelligence: A Qualitative Analysis of ChatGPT-3.5 Responses to Adolescents’ Questions on Romantic Relationships and Sexuality**  
*Archives of Sexual Behavior* · Couture et al. · [10.1007/s10508-026-03575-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-026-03575-2)  
→ **L10 Sex Education · L1**

Researchers collected 393 anonymized emails written in French and English by 14–16-year-olds to a recognized youth support organization, then put each question to ChatGPT-3.5 over two weeks in January 2024 using a netnography approach. 17.8% of the emails were flagged as potentially violating the content policy. Answers that did come through followed a consistent shape: an introduction restating the question, normalizing the experience and validating emotions; a list of recommendations; and a close encouraging help-seeking. The recommendations pushed authenticity, personal agency, self-care, open communication and seeking support.

**Why it matters:** Your lectures argue “porn is becoming the new sex education.” The sequel is already here. The **17.8% refusal rate** is the number that lands: the tool is least available exactly where teens most need it.

**Generative AI vs. Human Sources for Sexual Health Information: Credibility, Trust, and Behavioral Intentions in a U.S. College Sample**  
*Archives of Sexual Behavior* · Woerner et al. · [10.1007/s10508-026-03594-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-026-03594-z)  
→ **L10 Sex Education**

266 students at a southeastern U.S. university read three sexual-health passages — on sexual communication, body image, and sexual pleasure and safety — that were word-for-word identical across conditions. Only the stated author varied: generative AI, an academic expert, a community health professional, or a peer/social media influencer. The AI-attributed version scored lower on every outcome measured: perceived accuracy and clarity, warmth and helpfulness, trustworthiness, self-efficacy and willingness to act. Source effects on willingness to act were largest for pleasure and safety and smallest for communication, and held after controlling for students' own AI familiarity and attitudes.

**Why it matters:** A clean source-credibility experiment on your own student population, rhyming with the source-credibility material in PSY170 Lecture 11. Teachable conclusion: **AI works best paired with a human**.

**Use of Post-Coital Strategies to Prevent Pregnancy: Findings from a National Sample of Adolescents and Young Adults in the United States**  
*The Journal of Sex Research* · Steiner et al. · [10.1080/00224499.2026.2736663](https://doi.org/10.1080/00224499.2026.2736663)  
→ **L5 Fertility · L1** · also PSY157

A 2025 nationally representative online survey of 1,259 English-speaking 15–29-year-olds assigned female at birth, fielding a newly developed measure of post-coital contraceptive strategies for the first time. Among the 633 who had had penile-vaginal sex and were not pregnant, 25.0% used at least one post-coital strategy at last sex. Methods with established effectiveness — emergency contraception and the copper IUD — accounted for 8.9%, while 16.1% used a strategy with no medical basis, most commonly praying at 9.4%. Use varied by age, race and ethnicity, and number of contraceptive methods, and contraceptive knowledge was negatively associated with it.

**Why it matters:** Your Lecture 1 opens with a sexual-illiteracy poll. This is a 2025 nationally representative replacement, and **“9.4% prayed”** will land harder than any percentage currently on the slide.

**Problematic Pornography Use Among Swedish Adolescents: Characteristics and Clinical Implications**  
*Archives of Sexual Behavior* · Piwowar et al. · [10.1007/s10508-026-03544-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-026-03544-9)  
→ **L9 Sexual Behavior** · also PSY157

Problematic pornography use is the most common presentation of compulsive sexual behavior disorder, and many adults seeking help report that it began in adolescence — but adolescent prevalence had barely been measured. This cross-sectional study surveyed a representative sample of 3,106 Swedish third-year senior high school students, defining problematic use as self-reported difficulty refraining from or stopping. 10.4% reported it overall, with a large gender split: 18.8% of boys versus 3.9% of girls. It was associated with more time spent viewing, greater exposure to violent or otherwise concerning content, abusive experiences, mental health problems, risky behavior and non-suicidal self-injury.

**Why it matters:** Your slide says “1 in 3 teens believe porn has negatively influenced them,” which is a belief measure. This gives **measured prevalence, a large gender split and a correlate profile** from a representative sample.

**“It’s Like You’re Trying to Defuse a Bomb”: Fear, Responsibility, and Shame in Young Men’s Sexual Lives**  
*Journal of Sex & Marital Therapy* · Forrest et al. · [10.1080/0092623x.2026.2740624](https://doi.org/10.1080/0092623x.2026.2740624)  
→ **L9 Consent · L12**

Thematic analysis of 31 in-depth interviews with heterosexual men aged 18–32 who came of age in what the authors call the age of consent. The most striking finding was how rarely these men described enjoying sex at all. Three overlapping themes emerged: anxiety and vigilance during encounters, moral concern about causing harm to a partner, and shame arising as much from self-criticism as from any accusation. The authors describe an inward turn in which a cultural suspicion of men has been internalized, so the operative question becomes not only "does she want this?" but "am I the kind of man who could get this wrong?" One participant described sex as like trying to defuse a bomb.

**Why it matters:** An uncomfortable, high-quality counterpoint about what consent education can feel like from the inside. **Excellent discussion material for a mixed-gender classroom** precisely because it complicates the standard framing.

**Facial Asymmetry and Sexuality in Women**  
*Archives of Sexual Behavior* · Jaguszewska et al. · [10.1007/s10508-026-03527-w](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-026-03527-w)  
→ **L8 Symmetry · L3 Attraction** · also PSY157

Tests whether facial asymmetry predicts women's sexuality, measured both long-term via the three Sociosexual Orientation Inventory subscales (Behavior, Attitude, Desire) and currently via frequency and intensity items based on the Female Sexual Function Index. Simple correlations looked supportive: asymmetry related negatively to partners in the last year (ρ = −.23, 95% CI [−0.45, 0.01]), SOI Attitude (ρ = −.25) and sexual desire (ρ = −.21). But once place of residence — rural versus urban — was entered as a confounder, every one of those effects fell away (.42 < p < .91). The authors conclude that external conditions matter when studying correlates of facial asymmetry.

**Why it matters:** The most directly **corrective** item this month. Your symmetry slides state that symmetry signals health and drives mate selection; this shows how fast such correlates evaporate under one confound. It doubles as a research-methods moment for both courses.

> ⚠️ **Note.** The correlations did not survive a single confound control. The teachable point is the fragility, not an effect.

### PSY157 · Personal Relationships — 2

**The temporal dynamics of narcissism and aggression: Timescales, moderators, and generalizations in diverse samples**  
*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* · Liu et al. · [10.1037/pspp0000616](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000616)  
→ **L4 Narcissism · L8 Dark Triad** · also PSY170

Most evidence that narcissism breeds aggression comes from cross-sectional, between-person studies of Western convenience samples. This preregistered study used four large Chinese data sets instead: ambulatory assessment of 427 undergraduates and 775 offenders (19,331 and 42,820 observations) plus two longitudinal samples assessed four times over a year. Narcissism and aggression prospectively predicted each other at the momentary level but not the long-term level, and the reciprocal pattern largely replicated across students and offenders. Higher person-level aggression consistently strengthened the effect of narcissistic states on later aggression among offenders. The authors present this as the first within-person evidence for the dynamic.

**Why it matters:** Refines the blunt “narcissists are aggressive” claim into a **within-person, momentary** process — and directly answers the WEIRD-sample objection.

**Two Practices That Understate the Mental Health Risks Associated With Adolescent Social-Media Use**  
*Perspectives on Psychological Science* · Haidt & Rausch · [10.1177/17456916261482033](https://doi.org/10.1177/17456916261482033)  
→ **L7 Social Media**

Asks why researchers reading the same evidence disagree about whether social media harms adolescent mental health, and points to two analytic habits. First, studies usually report correlation coefficients alone rather than elevated risk, which is easier to misread. Second, they blend heterogeneous exposures (lumping digital activities together, television watching included), outcomes (depression with life satisfaction) and populations (girls with boys), then treat the weak aggregate association as evidence that specific risks are negligible. The authors show how sequential blending can turn a large, clinically relevant association into a negligible one, and urge reporting in terms of elevated risk and examining heterogeneity across platforms, usage levels and populations.

**Why it matters:** **Present as a live methodological dispute, not settled fact.** This is one side of an active argument about how to analyze the same body of evidence.

> ⚠️ **Note.** One side of an active methodological dispute. Present as contested, not settled.

### PSY170 · Industrial/Organizational Psychology — 7

**Psychology of Culture Change: The Power of Situations Returns**  
*Annual Review of Psychology* · Dasgupta · [10.1146/annurev-psych-030226-025335](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-030226-025335)  
→ **L3 Pre-employment**

Starts from the observation that most attempts at inclusive culture change have either not worked or actively backfired, and argues the common fault is treating culture as the sum of individual effort while underestimating situations. The review disaggregates situational power into four distinct types of force, drawing on social psychology and adjacent fields, and shows that some push beliefs, behaviors, interactions and relationships toward inclusion while others push the opposite way. A central aim is to trace how individual beliefs and behaviors connect upward into changes in local culture, and to mark where the research is still missing.

**Why it matters:** Your slide asks “Problems with equity?” and lists outcomes. This supplies the **mechanisms** behind why efforts stall or backfire.

**Conflict expression: A decade of group conflict research**  
*Current Opinion in Psychology* · Bendersky et al. · [10.1016/j.copsyc.2026.102468](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.copsyc.2026.102468)  
→ **L8 Groups**

Reviews a decade of work built on the conflict expression framework, which holds that what matters is not only what a conflict is about but how it is voiced. Expressions vary along two dimensions: directness and oppositional intensity. Three propositions follow — that the effects of a conflict depend on how it is expressed, that members of the same group perceive the same conflict differently, and that conflict moves negotiation dynamically through statements and the responses they draw. The review covers empirical tests of these propositions, methodological advances, and the framework's wider use as a foundation for conflict communication research.

**Why it matters:** A more current taxonomy than the five resolution styles alone.

**Consensus, Disagreements, and Open Questions in the Psychology of Misinformation**  
*Annual Review of Psychology* · Pennycook & Rand · [10.1146/annurev-psych-031626-100451](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-031626-100451)  
→ **L11 Persuasion**

Surveys a decade of misinformation research, separating settled ground from live dispute across definitions, measurement and theory. The authors argue susceptibility is best assessed as the ability to tell true claims from false ones rather than belief in falsehoods, and note that outright fake-news exposure online is rarer than commonly assumed even though misinformation more broadly construed remains a serious problem. They review the cognitive and socio-affective mechanisms behind susceptibility — repetition effects, weak analytic reasoning, political identity, information environments — and evaluate the main interventions: fact-checking, prebunking, psychological inoculation and accuracy prompts.

**Why it matters:** Speaks directly to your confirmation-bias and attitude-polarization slides.

**Why most stereotypes are negative**  
*Psychological Review* · Unkelbach et al. · [10.1037/rev0000647](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000647)  
→ **L2 Interview Bias · L4** · also PSY157

Offers a cognitive-ecological explanation for why stereotype content skews negative. Cognitively, people characterize a group by the attributes that distinguish it from other groups. Ecologically, positive social information is more frequent while negative information is more diverse — so negative attributes, being rarer and more varied, are more likely to distinguish one group from another. The authors demonstrate the predicted negativity across two studies and two existing data sets, then formalize the theory with simulations showing that rare attributes can be highly diagnostic of group membership while applying to only a minority of members. Notably, the account needs no motivational derogation, no essentialism and no general negativity bias.

**Why it matters:** A mechanism for the hiring-bias findings you already present as empirical facts.

**What We Talk About When We Talk About Empathy: Toward a Common Lexicon of Empathy-Related Constructs**  
*Perspectives on Psychological Science* · Davis et al. · [10.1177/17456916261443519](https://doi.org/10.1177/17456916261443519)  
→ **L13 EPOCH**

Fifteen empathy researchers set out to fix a problem that has dogged the field since the word was coined: "empathy" is used as an umbrella for a range of distinct processes, which muddles communication among scholars, practitioners and the public. They produced a shared lexicon of lower-order constructs — vicarious affect, emotional contagion, emotion co-sharing, sympathy/compassion, personal distress, perspective taking, accurate inference, mimicry, synchrony, projection and transportation. Their recommendation is that this more specific vocabulary be used routinely and the umbrella term sparingly.

**Why it matters:** Useful for sharpening what the **E in EPOCH** actually denotes.

**When and why women ask (or do not): Taking stock of research on gender differences in negotiation propensity, behavior, and outcomes**  
*Current Opinion in Psychology* · Bear et al. · [10.1016/j.copsyc.2026.102465](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.copsyc.2026.102465)  
→ **L3 · L11**

Takes stock of decades of evidence that women initiate fewer negotiations and perform less competitively, along with moderators such as structural ambiguity, representation role, and gender salience, and anticipated backlash as the proposed mechanism. Recent findings are mixed: gaps in negotiation behavior still replicate, but gaps in propensity to initiate and moderation by representation role often do not, and some studies find an outright reversal, with women more likely than men to negotiate in certain situations and female counterparts liked more, producing greater counterpart satisfaction. The authors flag statistical power as a likely source of inconsistency given the small-to-medium effects typical here, and conclude that performance gaps persist while propensity gaps have largely closed.

**Why it matters:** Good for teaching that **evidence moves** — not every classic finding holds.

**Revisiting the Queen Bee Phenomenon: Understanding Self–Group Distancing in Women and Other Groups as Strategic Compliance Within Unequal Systems**  
*Annual Review of Psychology* · Derks et al. · [10.1146/annurev-psych-021726-103815](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-021726-103815)  
→ **L3 · L10**

Revisits the Queen Bee phenomenon — senior women in male-dominated settings distancing from junior women, aligning with masculine norms and downplaying gender inequality. Rather than a character flaw, the review casts it as a way of coping with gendered organizational contexts and constrained advancement that provoke social identity threat. It is modeled as a socially embedded, self-reinforcing feedback loop: coping responses that may pay off for the individual in the short run while undermining solidarity, legitimizing inequality and reducing pressure for structural change. The authors situate it alongside related strategies including code-switching, identity bifurcation and assimilation, and argue for fixing systems rather than individuals.

**Why it matters:** Shifts the phenomenon from individual failing to structural response.

### PSY175 · Human Sexuality — 6

**Dyadic Associations between Body Appreciation and Sexual Function among Cisgender Heterosexual and Gender and Sexually Diverse Couples**  
*Journal of Sex & Marital Therapy* · Adlhoch-Mathé et al. · [10.1080/0092623x.2026.2744710](https://doi.org/10.1080/0092623x.2026.2744710)  
→ **L9 Orgasm Gap · L2**

151 couples completed measures of body appreciation and sexual function, analyzed with an actor–partner interdependence moderation model, in a sample deliberately including gender and sexually diverse couples who face elevated risk of sexual difficulty through minority stress. A person's body appreciation was negatively related to their own sexual function difficulties — and to their partner's difficulties, but only when that partner was heterosexual. **Why it matters:** **Dyadic evidence for the body-shame mechanism** you already invoke to explain the orgasm gap — and it includes gender-diverse couples.

**Mediators and Moderators of Treatment Outcome Following Online Mindfulness and Cognitive-Behavioral Treatment for Female Sexual Interest/Arousal Disorder**  
*The Journal of Sex Research* · Brotto et al. · [10.1080/00224499.2026.2743644](https://doi.org/10.1080/00224499.2026.2743644)  
→ **L9 · L8**

Examines what actually drives improvement in eSense, a digital tool delivering mindfulness-based therapy or CBT for female sexual interest/arousal disorder. 129 women were randomized to an arm (mean age 44.8 in MBT, 41.9 in CBT) and assessed at baseline, mid-treatment, post-treatment and six-month follow-up. Decreases in depressive and PTSD symptoms accompanied improvements in desire and distress in the mindfulness arm, while reductions in avoidance of sexual activity accompanied improvement in both arms. Higher treatment expectations predicted greater gains in the CBT arm only, whereas partner involvement, homework completion and number of modules finished predicted gains in both.

**Why it matters:** Mechanism detail for your vaginismus and sexual-dysfunction treatment slides — and **avoidance reduction as the common pathway** is the teachable point.

**Detransition, Posttraumatic Stress Disorder, and Moral Injury**  
*Archives of Sexual Behavior* · Clayton & McDeavitt · [10.1007/s10508-026-03669-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-026-03669-x)
